What is a Nash Equilibrium?

In any strategic situation, each player chooses a strategy. A Nash Equilibrium is reached when no player can improve their outcome by changing their strategy — assuming everyone else keeps theirs the same.

Think of it as a stable resting point: you look at what the other player is doing, and you think "given their choice, I wouldn't want to switch."

It was developed by mathematician John Nash in 1950 and is the most important concept in game theory.

Formal definition

A strategy profile (s₁, s₂) is a Nash Equilibrium if, for every player i, sᵢ is a best response to the other player's strategy — meaning no player can do better by deviating unilaterally.

Three things to remember

Stable, not optimal. A Nash Equilibrium is stable — nobody wants to deviate. But it doesn't mean it's the best outcome for everyone.

Unilateral deviation. The key word is "unilaterally" — one player changes while the other stays put. If both change together, that's a different question.

Can be multiple or none. A game can have zero, one, or many Nash Equilibria in pure strategies. If there are none, you need mixed strategies.

The Prisoner's Dilemma
Two suspects are interrogated separately. Each can either stay Silent or Confess. The numbers show years in prison — lower is better.
P2: Silent P2: Confess
P1: Silent −1, −1 −10, 0
P1: Confess 0, −10 −5, −5 ✓
How to find it — step by step
Step 1 — Player 1's best responses

Look at each column and find P1's highest payoff (first number).

If P2 plays Silent: P1 gets −1 (Silent) or 0 (Confess) → Confess wins
If P2 plays Confess: P1 gets −10 (Silent) or −5 (Confess) → Confess wins
Step 2 — Player 2's best responses

Look at each row and find P2's highest payoff (second number).

If P1 plays Silent: P2 gets −1 (Silent) or 0 (Confess) → Confess wins
If P1 plays Confess: P2 gets −10 (Silent) or −5 (Confess) → Confess wins
Step 3 — Find the intersection

The Nash Equilibrium is where both players are playing their best response at the same time.

Both players best-respond with Confess → Nash Equilibrium is (Confess, Confess) = −5, −5

The paradox

Notice that (Silent, Silent) gives both players −1, which is better than −5. But it's not stable — if you're both silent, each player is tempted to confess and get 0 instead of −1.

This is why the Nash Equilibrium isn't always the "best" outcome — it's just the outcome where nobody has an incentive to change alone.
